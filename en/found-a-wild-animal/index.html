Found a wild animal. What should I do?

Answer a few questions for guidance tailored to the situation. In an emergency, do not wait to finish — use the contact links.

What matters right now

Keep your distance. Do not give food, water or medication. Do not risk your own safety.

Who to contact?

Contact an appropriate wildlife organisation or regional environmental inspectorate (RIEW). If people are in immediate danger, call 112. Marina Sanctuary is a developing project, not a 24-hour emergency centre.

Detailed guidance by situation

Read the guidance without using the interactive assistant. When in doubt, contact a specialist.

Prioritise human safety

Do not attempt a rescue that puts you or others at risk.

  1. Move away. Call 112 if people are in immediate danger.
  2. Describe the location and hazard. Do not enter the road or touch electrical wires.
  3. After a bite or scratch, wash the wound with soap and water and seek medical attention promptly.
The bird needs specialist assessment

Do not wait for visible wounds after a collision or attack.

  1. Contact a specialist. Do not handle raptors, storks, herons or large birds.
  2. Only if a specialist confirms it is safe, place a small bird in a ventilated cardboard box with clean paper lining in a quiet, dark place.
  3. Do not give food, water by mouth, milk or medication. Do not treat wounds or manipulate limbs.
  4. Record the precise location, time, visible symptoms and any cat or dog contact. Take a photo from a safe distance.
The nestling may need to return to its nest

If uninjured, its best chance is often with its parents.

  1. Look for the original nest without climbing into danger.
  2. Ask a specialist about safe reunification. If the nest is inaccessible, or the bird is cold, weak or injured, seek help promptly.
  3. Do not give food, water by mouth, milk or medication. Do not treat wounds or manipulate limbs.
  4. Record the precise location, time, visible symptoms and any cat or dog contact. Take a photo from a safe distance.
It may be a normal fledgling

Many fledglings leave the nest before flying well. Their parents continue feeding them.

  1. Move away, keep pets indoors and observe without disturbing the parents.
  2. Do not remove it from the area. If in danger, ask about moving it to a safe spot very close by.
  3. If weak, injured, touched by a cat/dog, or if species is uncertain (for example a swift), contact a specialist.
Observe and ask for advice if uncertain

An adult bird on the ground is not necessarily in trouble, but some species require special assessment.

  1. Do not chase it to test flight or throw it into the air.
  2. If it cannot move normally, is lethargic or has collided with something, contact a specialist.
  3. Record the precise location, time, visible symptoms and any cat or dog contact. Take a photo from a safe distance.
Leave healthy young wildlife where it is

A lone fawn or leveret is usually not evidence of orphaning.

  1. Leave promptly and keep people and dogs away.
  2. Do not remain nearby waiting for the mother; your presence may deter her.
  3. If the mother is known dead, the young is injured or in danger, contact a specialist before intervening.
Ask about safe reunification

Young mammals have different needs by species and age. There is no universal waiting time.

  1. Do not move it far away. Note where it was found and whether there is a disturbed nest or dead mother.
  2. If cold, weak, injured or in danger, seek specialist help promptly. Follow their instructions for warmth and temporary containment.
  3. Do not give food, water by mouth, milk or medication. Do not treat wounds or manipulate limbs.
  4. Record the precise location, time, visible symptoms and any cat or dog contact. Take a photo from a safe distance.
Specialist assistance is needed

Do not attempt to treat or capture a wild mammal yourself.

  1. Keep your distance. Even small animals can bite. Tell the specialist the species, size and behaviour.
  2. Only under specific safe containment instructions, use a suitable ventilated container away from noise, children and pets.
  3. Do not give food, water by mouth, milk or medication. Do not treat wounds or manipulate limbs.
  4. Record the precise location, time, visible symptoms and any cat or dog contact. Take a photo from a safe distance.
Do not approach the predator

Even a young or apparently calm animal may defend itself.

  1. Do not chase, capture or feed it.
  2. If in its normal habitat with no signs of distress, leave it alone. A lone juvenile does not prove orphaning.
  3. If injured, trapped or possibly distressed, contact a specialist or RIEW with a location and distant photo.
Leave the animal undisturbed

Your description gives no clear indication that intervention is needed. This is not a diagnosis.

  1. Move away and keep pets away.
  2. Do not feed or relocate healthy wildlife.
  3. Seek advice if new signs of injury, unusual behaviour or danger appear.
Bats require specialist handling

Do not touch a bat with bare hands, even if it appears lifeless.

  1. Keep people and pets away. Do not attempt to make it fly.
  2. For a grounded, indoor or distressed bat, seek a bat specialist through a rescue centre or RIEW.
  3. If bitten, scratched or possibly exposed to saliva, wash the area and seek urgent medical assessment. Contact a vet if a pet was exposed.
Assess the situation with a specialist

A turtle or tortoise on land or near water may be in its natural habitat.

  1. Do not relocate it or put it in water if the species is unknown.
  2. For a cracked shell, bleeding, attack or immediate danger, contact the tortoise rescue centre.
  3. If on a road, do not expose yourself to traffic. Seek safe guidance for the case.
Do not handle or capture the reptile

Do not rely on appearance to determine whether a snake is venomous.

  1. Keep your distance. Do not pursue or kill it.
  2. If indoors, injured or in immediate danger, contact RIEW or an appropriate specialist.
  3. After a snakebite, seek emergency medical care.
Do not take risks with unidentified wildlife

Distance and accurate information are most helpful.

  1. Do not handle it, even if it seems calm.
  2. Record the precise location, time, visible symptoms and any cat or dog contact. Take a photo from a safe distance.
  3. Contact RIEW or a wildlife organisation and share the information.
